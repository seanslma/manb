# manager mistakes
James EveringhamJames Everingham: VP of Engineering, Meta

As a manager, I've made my fair share of mistakes. When I fixed these behaviors, the folks who worked for me could achieve much more. Below are some of the top mistakes from early management career:
- **Unaware of my influence**: I loved brainstorming ideas with my team, but I later realized that my position of power led them to focus solely on my suggestions. Focusing on the outcomes and removing myself from the discussion made the team much more creative.
- **Protected my team from failing**: I used to stop my team and suggest a different path when I thought their ideas would lead to failure. When a mentor told me to stop and let them fail, I learned I was only right sometimes. Their ideas were, more often than not, better than mine.
- **Took on work that "wasn't fun" myself**: I would sign up for work I couldn't imagine someone would enjoy. A manager once called me out, frustrated, asking why I didn't offer it to them. I made the mistake of thinking others think like me. I now offer up every task.
- **Was a lousy load balancer**: I used to delegate work to those I thought had the most bandwidth rather than the most interest or qualifications. When I stopped this and started loading reports until they pushed back, the team became more productive, morale increased, and careers flourished.
- **Didn't deputize my reports**: I used to think I needed to be the decision-maker/owner for initiatives that spanned the broader organization. However, once I started assigning those jobs to reports and clarified that they had authority, my team's capabilities and morale grew.
- **Spent too much time with underperformers**: I thought the best way to increase team capability was to focus on struggling reports. It felt like the right thing, but others felt ignored. Team productivity took off when I flipped the equation, giving top performers 70% of my time.
- **Couldn't control my emotions**: My lack of emotional control led to poor decisions, poor communication, high stress, and bad role modeling. Gaining control and remaining calm in any situation allowed my team to trust that I was objective and not emotionally biased.
- **Going for 100% consensus**: I wanted everyone to agree on decisions, but getting everyone to agree is almost impossible. As a result, I was slowing down and frustrating everyone by not making decisions quickly. So, I now go for 80% alignment and expect others to commit.
- **Burned out my team with my own poor work-life balance**: I was the first person my team saw when they came in and the last when they left. I wanted my team to know I was working hard, too, but they were mirroring me, and I was burning everyone out.
- **Didn't double down on my top performers**: Some people are incredibly capable. Not recognizing this and failing to give top performers more opportunities held them back. Placing big bets on my stars accelerated their careers, increased team impact, and furthered my career.
